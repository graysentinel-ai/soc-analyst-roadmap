1. Learning objectives

By the end of this module, you should be able to:

  • Explain the purpose of Identity and Access Management.
  • Differentiate authentication from authorisation.
  • Explain MFA, SSO, federation and passwordless authentication.
  • Understand RBAC, ABAC and least privilege.
  • Describe the joiner, mover and leaver identity lifecycle.
  • Identify risks involving excessive permissions and stale accounts.
  • Review sign-in and access records for suspicious patterns.
  • Prepare an evidence-based access review report.
Core principle: A valid login does not automatically mean a user should have access to every resource. Authentication establishes identity; authorisation determines permitted actions.

2. IAM fundamentals

Identity and Access Management is the set of policies, processes and technologies used to manage digital identities and control access to systems, applications and information.

Identity

A digital representation of a person, service, device or other entity.

Account

A record used by an identity to access a particular system or service.

Authentication

Verifying that an identity is who or what it claims to be.

Authorisation

Determining what an authenticated identity is allowed to do.

Permission

A specific allowed action, such as read, write, approve or administer.

Access review

Checking whether assigned access remains appropriate and approved.

Why IAM matters

  • Reduces unauthorised access to systems and data.
  • Limits the impact of compromised accounts.
  • Supports accountability through identity-linked activity records.
  • Helps remove access when a person changes role or leaves.
  • Supports compliance and organisational access policies.

3. Authentication

Authentication verifies an identity before access is granted. Different methods rely on different types of evidence.

Authentication factor categories

Something you know

A password, PIN or other memorised secret.

Something you have

A hardware security key or registered authenticator device.

Something you are

A biometric characteristic such as a fingerprint or face match.

Context and signals

Device state, location or behaviour may inform risk-based access decisions, but are not automatically independent authentication factors.

Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)

MFA requires evidence from at least two different authentication factor categories. Using two passwords does not provide two independent factors.

MethodExampleSecurity consideration
PasswordMemorised secretProtect against reuse, phishing and weak credentials
Authenticator appTime-based one-time passwordProtect account recovery and enrolment processes
Push approvalApprove a sign-in notificationUsers can be targeted with repeated approval requests
Hardware security keyFIDO2 security keyPhishing-resistant when correctly implemented
BiometricsFingerprint or face verificationSecure device enrolment and recovery are important
PasskeyCryptographic credential tied to a relying partyUse supported platform and account recovery protections

Single Sign-On (SSO)

SSO allows a user to authenticate through an identity provider and access multiple connected applications without separately entering credentials for each application.

Federated identity

Federation allows separate systems or organisations to trust identity assertions from an agreed identity provider. Common technologies include SAML and OpenID Connect.

SSO improves user experience but concentrates trust in the identity provider. Protect privileged identity accounts, authentication methods, recovery procedures and application integrations.

4. Authorisation and access decisions

After authentication, the system determines whether the identity can perform a requested action on a resource.

Common permission types

PermissionMeaningExample
ReadView informationOpen a report
WriteCreate or modify informationEdit a record
DeleteRemove informationDelete an obsolete file
ExecuteRun a program or operationExecute an approved script
ApproveAuthorise a defined actionApprove a purchase request
AdministerManage a system or its configurationManage user accounts

Least privilege

Give an identity only the minimum permissions needed to perform its authorised responsibilities. Review permissions when responsibilities change and remove access that is no longer required.

Separation of duties

Divide sensitive responsibilities so that one individual cannot complete a critical process without the required independent review or approval.

Example: The person who creates a payment request should not automatically be the only person authorised to approve and release that payment.

5. Access control models

ModelHow access is determinedExample
RBAC Permissions are assigned to roles, and users receive roles. Finance Analyst role can read finance reports.
ABAC Access is evaluated using attributes and policy rules. Allow access when department, device and data classification meet policy.
DAC Resource owners can decide who receives access. A document owner shares a file with another user.
MAC Access is controlled through centrally defined security labels and policy. Access to information based on approved clearance and classification.

RBAC example

Role: Security Analyst
Permissions:
- Read security alerts
- View investigation records
- Create investigation notes

Role: Security Administrator
Permissions:
- Manage security configurations
- Manage approved security integrations
- Administer security platform access

Role: Auditor
Permissions:
- Read audit records
- View policy evidence
- Export approved reports
A role should not become a shortcut for excessive permissions. Review the actual permissions behind each role, not just its name.

6. Identity lifecycle management

Identity access should change as a person's relationship with an organisation changes.

Joiner

Create the identity, verify the request, assign approved access and record ownership.

Mover

Change permissions when a person changes role, team or responsibility.

Leaver

Disable or remove access when the person leaves, according to approved procedures.

Periodic review

Confirm that existing access remains appropriate and supported by an owner.

Example lifecycle process

  1. Receive an approved identity or access request.
  2. Verify the person's identity and employment or engagement status.
  3. Confirm the business owner and required access.
  4. Assign access based on approved roles and policies.
  5. Record the approval and provisioning activity.
  6. Review access after role or responsibility changes.
  7. Disable or remove access when no longer authorised.
  8. Retain appropriate audit evidence according to policy.
Delayed offboarding can leave active accounts behind. However, do not delete records or accounts without considering legal retention, investigation holds, ownership transfer and organisational policy.

Privileged accounts

Privileged accounts can change security settings, manage users, access sensitive data or control infrastructure. They should receive stronger oversight than ordinary user accounts.

  • Maintain an inventory of privileged accounts.
  • Assign a named business and technical owner.
  • Use separate administrative identities where appropriate.
  • Require strong authentication and controlled access.
  • Review privileged activity and permission changes.
  • Remove unnecessary or dormant privileged access.

11. Identity investigation workflow

  1. Define the question: Identify the account, resource or access event under review.
  2. Confirm ownership: Identify the identity owner and business approver.
  3. Review authentication: Check sign-in time, source, method, result and MFA details.
  4. Review permissions: Identify assigned roles, groups and effective access.
  5. Check lifecycle status: Validate employment, engagement and role information.
  6. Correlate events: Compare sign-in, permission change and resource access records.
  7. Assess impact: Identify the resources and actions available to the identity.
  8. Document findings: Separate facts, interpretations and unknowns.
  9. Recommend action: Route changes through the authorised approval process.
Useful investigation questions:
  • Who requested the access?
  • Who approved it?
  • When was it provisioned?
  • What permissions were actually assigned?
  • Does the identity still need that access?
  • Was the access used in an expected way?

12. Access review report

Prepare a report that makes each recommendation traceable to evidence.

  1. Review scope: Systems, identities and review period.
  2. Identity inventory: Accounts, owners and current status.
  3. Access inventory: Roles, groups and permissions.
  4. Approval evidence: Request, owner and authorisation records.
  5. Authentication review: Relevant sign-in and MFA observations.
  6. Exceptions: Excessive, stale, orphaned or undocumented access.
  7. Risk assessment: Potential impact and confidence.
  8. Recommendations: Specific changes requiring approval.
  9. Evidence references: Record identifiers and supporting extracts.

Example finding

Observation:
The synthetic access inventory shows an account assigned
identity-platform administrator permissions while its stated
role is Security Intern. The access owner is not recorded.

Assessment:
The assignment may exceed the user's documented responsibilities.
The available records do not establish whether the access was
approved through another process.

Recommended next steps:
Identify the business and technical owner, locate the access
request and approval, validate current responsibilities, and
route any required permission change through the approved process.
